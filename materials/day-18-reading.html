Day 18: Allergies and Drug Interactions

Welcome to Day 18 of your pharmacy technician training program. Patient safety relies heavily on accurate allergy documentation and vigilant screening for drug-drug, drug-food, and OTC interactions. Today's curriculum covers immunologic drug hypersensitivity versus adverse drug events, beta-lactam and sulfonamide cross-reactivity, pharmacokinetic (CYP450 enzymes) and pharmacodynamic interaction mechanisms, high-risk OTC interactions, and technician protocols for handling allergy alerts and pharmacist clinical handoffs.

Understanding Immune Allergies vs. Adverse Side Effects

Immunologic hypersensitivity mechanisms, clinical presentation, and accurate pharmacy profile documentation.

1. True Drug Allergy (Hypersensitivity)

A true drug allergy is an immunologically mediated reaction where the body's immune system recognizes a medication or its metabolite as a foreign antigen. Type I hypersensitivity reactions are mediated by Immunoglobulin E (IgE) antibodies bound to mast cells and basophils, triggering rapid release of histamine and inflammatory cytokines.

True IgE Allergy Symptoms
  • Urticaria (Hives) and severe pruritus (itching)
  • Angioedema (swelling of lips, tongue, or throat)
  • Bronchospasm, wheezing, dyspnea (shortness of breath)
  • Anaphylaxis: Life-threatening systemic reaction with hypotension and airway compromise
Side Effects / Intolerance
  • Nausea, mild stomach upset, or diarrhea (e.g., Erythromycin)
  • Dry cough from ACE inhibitors (Lisinopril)
  • Drowsiness from antihistamines
  • Headache or mild dizziness

2. Differentiating Allergy from Side Effects in Practice

Patients often report predictable side effects as "allergies" (e.g., "I am allergic to codeine because it makes me nauseous"). Technicians must inquire about the specific reaction to document the profile correctly:

Feature True IgE Allergic Reaction Adverse Side Effect / Intolerance
Mechanism Immune-mediated (IgE antibodies, histamine) Pharmacologic action of drug; non-immunologic
Predictability Unpredictable; independent of dose Predictable based on mechanism; dose-dependent
Re-exposure Risk Strictly contraindicated; risk of fatal anaphylaxis May manage with dose reduction, food, or alt agent
Documentation ALLERGY: Drug + Specific Symptom (e.g. Hives) ADVERSE REACTION: Drug + Symptom (e.g. Upset Stomach)

Major Drug Allergies & Chemical Cross-Reactivity

High-risk drug allergen classes, structural cross-reactivity, and emergency management.

1. Major Drug Allergen Classes

Certain classes of medications account for the majority of drug-induced allergic reactions in clinical practice:

Drug Class / Agent Representative Examples Allergic Manifestations Cross-Reactivity Concerns
Penicillins ($\beta$-lactams) Amoxicillin, Ampicillin, Penicillin V, Augmentin Hives, rash, fever, anaphylaxis 1st generation Cephalosporins ($\approx 3\text{--}5\%$ risk); Carbapenems (low)
Sulfonamide Antibiotics Sulfamethoxazole (in Bactrim / Septra) Severe skin rash, Stevens-Johnson Syndrome (SJS), fever Non-arylamine sulfas (thiazides, sulfonylureas) have low cross-reactivity but require caution
NSAIDs & Aspirin Ibuprofen, Naproxen, Aspirin Urticaria, bronchospasm, Samter's triad (asthma + nasal polyps) Cross-reactivity across all non-selective NSAIDs
Anticonvulsants Phenytoin, Carbamazepine, Lamotrigine DRESS syndrome, Stevens-Johnson Syndrome (SJS) Cross-reactivity among aromatic antiepileptics

2. Beta-Lactam Cross-Reactivity Risk Evaluation

Penicillins and Cephalosporins share a core $\beta$-lactam ring structural feature. Historically, cross-reactivity was estimated at $10\%$, but modern immunological studies demonstrate that true cross-reactivity between penicillins and 1st-generation cephalosporins (e.g., Cefazolin, Cephalexin) is approximately $3\text{--}5\%$, and even lower ($< 1\%$) for 3rd and 4th generation cephalosporins (e.g., Ceftriaxone, Cefepime).

Cross-Reactivity Probability Model
$\text{Risk}_{\text{1st Gen Ceph}} \approx 3\text{--}5\%$ (if true Penicillin anaphylaxis history)
$\text{Risk}_{\text{3rd/4th Gen Ceph}} < 1\%$ (due to distinct R1 side-chain structure)
$\text{Risk}_{\text{Aztreonam}} \approx 0\%$ (Monobactam - safe except with Ceftazidime allergy)

3. Anaphylaxis Emergency Recognition & Management

Anaphylaxis Emergency Red Flags & Treatment:
  • Rapid onset (minutes) of airway tightness, wheezing, swelling of mouth/throat, severe hypotension.
  • First-line Emergency Treatment: Intramuscular (IM) Epinephrine (EpiPen: $0.3\text{ mg}$ for adults, $0.15\text{ mg}$ for pediatrics).
  • Technicians must alert the pharmacist and call 911 immediately if a patient exhibits symptoms in the pharmacy.

Drug-Drug Interactions: Pharmacokinetics & Pharmacodynamics

Cytochrome P450 enzyme mechanisms, absorption chelation, additive vs. antagonistic effects.

1. Pharmacokinetic (PK) Interactions: CYP450 Enzymes

Pharmacokinetic interactions alter how the body absorbs, distributes, metabolizes, or excretes a drug ($ADME$). The primary hepatic enzyme system responsible for drug metabolism is the Cytochrome P450 (CYP450) family (e.g., CYP3A4, CYP2D6, CYP2C9).

CYP450 Enzyme Inhibitors

Inhibitors block enzyme metabolism, causing substrate drug concentrations to increase ($C_{\max} \uparrow$).

Risk: Drug Toxicity / Overdose
Examples: Ketoconazole, Erythromycin, Clarithromycin, Ritonavir.
CYP450 Enzyme Inducers

Inducers speed up enzyme metabolism, causing substrate drug concentrations to decrease ($C_{\max} \downarrow$).

Risk: Therapeutic Failure / Subtherapeutic Levels
Examples: Rifampin, Carbamazepine, Phenytoin, St. John's Wort.

2. Mathematical Model of Enzyme Kinetics & Clearance

Drug clearance ($CL$) and steady-state concentration ($C_{ss}$) are related to metabolism rate:

$$C_{ss} = \frac{\text{Dose} \times F}{CL \times \tau}$$
Where $F$ is bioavailability, $CL$ is clearance, and $\tau$ is dosing interval.
• When a CYP Inhibitor decreases $CL$, concentration $C_{ss}$ increases proportionally ($\uparrow$), risking toxicity.
• When a CYP Inducer increases $CL$, concentration $C_{ss}$ decreases proportionally ($\downarrow$), leading to treatment failure.

3. Pharmacodynamic (PD) Interactions

Pharmacodynamic interactions occur when drugs act on the same receptor or physiological system:

Interaction Type Mechanism Description Clinical Example
Additive / Synergistic Two drugs produce combined enhanced effect ($1+1 \ge 2$) Combining Opioids (Oxycodone) + Benzodiazepines (Alprazolam) $\rightarrow$ Severe CNS & respiratory depression (Black Box Warning)
Antagonistic One drug blocks or opposes the effect of another Naloxone (Opioid Antagonist) reversing Morphine overdose; Albuterol ($\beta_2$-agonist) combined with Non-selective Beta-blockers (Propranolol)

OTC Medication & Food-Drug Interactions

Critical dietary interactions, cation chelation, and high-risk OTC drug interference.

1. Major Food-Drug Interactions

Dietary components can significantly alter drug absorption, metabolism, and pharmacodynamic response:

Food / Beverage Interacting Prescription Drug Interaction Mechanism Clinical Effect & Safety Advice
Grapefruit Juice Statins (Atorvastatin, Simvastatin), CCBs (Nifedipine) Inhibits intestinal CYP3A4 enzyme Increases drug absorption $\rightarrow$ Toxicity risk (Rhabdomyolysis / severe hypotension)
Vitamin K (Leafy Greens) Warfarin (Coumadin) Pharmacodynamic antagonism of clotting factors Decreases Warfarin efficacy $\rightarrow$ Risk of stroke/clots. Keep Vit K intake consistent!
Dairy / Calcium Products Fluoroquinolones (Ciprofloxacin), Tetracyclines (Doxycycline) Polyvalent Cation Chelation in GI tract Forms insoluble complexes $\rightarrow$ Prevents antibiotic absorption. Separate by 2 hours.
Tyramine-Rich Foods (Aged Cheese, Wine) MAO Inhibitors (Phenelzine) Inhibition of tyramine breakdown Severe Hypertensive Crisis (life-threatening BP spike)

2. High-Risk OTC Drug Interactions

NSAIDs + Antihypertensives Ibuprofen / Naproxen + ACE Inhibitors or Diuretics. Causes fluid retention, reduces BP drug efficacy, and risks acute renal impairment ("Triple Whammy").
Antacids + Levothyroxine Calcium / Magnesium Antacids + Thyroid Hormone. Antacids bind thyroid hormone in GI tract; separate administration by at least 4 hours.
St. John's Wort + Oral Contraceptives / SSRIs Herbal OTC Supplement + Rx drugs. CYP3A4 induction leads to contraceptive failure; serotonin syndrome with SSRIs.

Module 5 • 20 Minutes Target

Role-Playing: Handling Allergies and Interaction Alerts

Technician intake workflow, electronic pharmacy software alerts, and clinical pharmacist handoffs.

1. Technician Profile Intake Protocol

During patient intake, technicians must actively verify and document complete allergy information in the electronic pharmacy management system:

Standard Allergy Intake Procedure
1. Ask Open-Ended Question: "What drug or food allergies do you have?"
2. Clarify Specific Reaction: "What happens when you take that medication? (Hives, swelling, nausea?)"
3. Document Specific Agent: Select specific chemical entity in dispensing software (not generic 'antibiotics').
4. Update Profile Stamp: Record date, time, and technician initials.

2. Managing Electronic Drug Interaction Alerts

Pharmacy software automatically screens for Drug-Drug (DDI), Drug-Allergy (DAI), and Drug-Disease interactions. When a high-severity alert pops up:

Technician Responsibilities
  • Never override or bypass a Level 1 (Major/Severe) clinical interaction alert independently.
  • Verify profile data entry accuracy (confirm correct drug name and strength were entered).
  • Print or queue the alert soft-stop for pharmacist clinical review.
Pharmacist Clinical Consultation
  • Pharmacist evaluates therapeutic appropriateness and risk vs. benefit ratio.
  • Pharmacist contacts prescribing physician if therapy modification is required.
  • Pharmacist counsels patient on interaction monitoring or administration spacing.
