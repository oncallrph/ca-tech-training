Module 1 • 20 Minutes Target

Pharmacy Station Workflow & Queue Management

Understanding sequential prescription flow, station-by-station responsibilities, and preventing operational bottlenecks.

1. Sequential Prescription Flow Model

In retail and health-system outpatient pharmacies, prescription processing moves through a strict, linear pipeline to ensure safety, traceability, and speed:

1 Drop-Off / Intake
Receiving paper/e-prescriptions, verifying 2 patient identifiers, scanning documents, and assessing promised turnaround time.
2 Data Entry & Billing
Transcribing prescriber, drug, sig, quantity, refills, and submitting third-party insurance adjudication (resolving rejections).
3 Pharmacist DUR Check
Drug Utilization Review (DUR): Pharmacist checks clinical appropriateness, allergies, interactions, and dosage safety.
4 Filling / Dispensing
Selecting stock bottle using NDC barcode match, counting/measuring, packaging in vial, and affixing prescription label.
5 Pharmacist Verification
Final safety check: Pharmacist verifies physical product, label accuracy, stock bottle, and hard-copy image.
6 Will Call / Pick-Up
Storing completed order in bin system, customer check-out, identity confirmation, payment, and offering pharmacist counseling.

2. Preventing Bottlenecks & Managing Workflow Queues

A workflow bottleneck occurs when prescriptions accumulate at one station faster than they can be processed. Common causes and corrective actions include:

Workflow Queue / Station Primary Root Cause of Bottleneck Technician Mitigation Strategy
Data Entry / Adjudication Insurance claim rejections (prior authorization, refill too soon, invalid ID). Flag rejected claims immediately; move to resolution queue without stopping input of clean orders.
Filling Station Disorganized stock, unreturned inventory bottles, missing supplies. Maintain continuous shelf return discipline; keep top fast-mover medications accessible.
Pick-Up / Will Call Long counter lines, misplaced completed prescription bags. Use barcode bin verification systems; perform regular return-to-stock maintenance for uncollected bags ($> 14$ days).

Module 2 • 25 Minutes Target

Excellence in Customer Service & Professional Communication

Communication channels, active listening, HIPAA privacy standards during transactions, and telephone etiquette.

1. Core Pillars of Professional Pharmacy Communication

Patients visiting a pharmacy are often ill, stressed, or under financial strain. Technicians must combine technical accuracy with compassionate communication:

Verbal & Non-Verbal Skills
  • Active Listening: Maintain eye contact, nod, and paraphrase concerns without interrupting.
  • Tone of Voice: Speak clearly, calmly, and at a moderate volume to maintain a reassuring atmosphere.
  • Body Language: Open posture, uncrossed arms, and attentive eye contact convey willingness to help.
HIPAA Counter Privacy
  • Low-Volume Speaking: Avoid calling out drug names, conditions, or full SSNs loudly across the counter.
  • Screen Shielding: Position computer monitors away from public line-of-sight.
  • Verification: Always verify two unique identifiers (e.g., Full Legal Name + Date of Birth) before dispensing.

2. Professional Telephone Etiquette

Telephone interactions create a critical first impression of the pharmacy's clinical professionalism:

Standard Telephone Greeting Protocol:
  1. Pharmacy Name & Department: "Thank you for calling South Coast Mission Pharmacy..."
  2. Self-Introduction & Role: "This is Alex, registered pharmacy technician..."
  3. Offer Assistance: "How may I help you today?"
  4. Triage & Holding: Always ask permission before placing a caller on hold ("May I put you on a brief hold while I look up your refill?"), and limit holds to under 60 seconds without checking back.

Module 3 • 20 Minutes Target

Medication Counseling & Scope of Practice Boundaries

OBRA '90 legal mandates, distinguishing technician duties from mandatory pharmacist clinical consultation.

1. The Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1990 (OBRA '90)

OBRA '90 revolutionized outpatient pharmacy practice by mandating that pharmacists perform a Drug Utilization Review (DUR) and offer patient counseling for Medicaid recipients, which state boards (including California) expanded to cover all patients:

Key OBRA '90 Area Legal Standard & Requirement Technician's Administrative Duty
Mandatory Offer to Counsel An offer for pharmacist consultation must be made on all new prescriptions or changed dosages. Ask the patient: "Do you have any questions for the pharmacist today?" and route them to the pharmacist if yes.
Patient Profile Maintenance Must maintain record of name, address, age, gender, allergies, drug reactions, and history. Accurately record patient demographic, allergy, and insurance updates in the computer system.
Prospective DUR Screening for therapeutic duplication, drug-disease contraindications, interactions, dosage errors. Alert the pharmacist whenever system clinical DUR warning pop-ups trigger.

2. Scope of Practice: Tech vs. Pharmacist Duties

Strict boundaries exist between administrative execution (technician) and clinical evaluation/counseling (pharmacist):

Permissible Technician Duties
  • Entering prescriptions and billing insurance claims.
  • Counting tablets, placing labels on vials.
  • Asking if the patient has questions for the pharmacist.
  • Checking out completed orders at the register.
  • Providing price quotes and restocking shelf inventory.
Strictly Prohibited for Technicians
  • Explaining how to take a medication or describing side effects.
  • Recommending OTC medications for patient symptoms.
  • Interpreting clinical lab results or dosage appropriateness.
  • Evaluating potential drug-drug or food-drug interactions.
  • Overriding clinical DUR alerts without pharmacist review.

Module 4 • 20 Minutes Target

Managing Difficult Customers & Conflict Resolution (LAST Method)

De-escalation tactics, managing long wait times, stock shortages, insurance rejections, and the LAST framework.

1. The LAST Framework for Conflict Resolution

When dealing with upset or anxious patients, technicians should apply the structured LAST de-escalation framework:

L
Listen
Give full attention without interrupting. Allow the patient to vent their frustration.
A
Apologize
Offer a sincere empathy statement ("I apologize for the delay and frustration this caused.").
S
Solve
Offer concrete solutions (e.g., partial fill, calling insurance, expediting queue).
T
Thank
Thank the patient for their patience and understanding while you resolve the issue.

2. Handling Common Pharmacy Conflict Scenarios

Practical strategies for resolving high-frequency pharmacy counter disputes:

Conflict Scenario Root Cause Professional Action & Communication
High Copay / Insurance Rejection Deductible, prior auth, or non-formulations. Explain that insurance sets the price; check for discount programs or suggest pharmacist formulary alternatives.
Out-of-Stock Medication Supply chain delay or unexpected demand surge. Offer partial fill (if allowed), check sister store inventory, or place emergency order for next morning delivery.
Prescription Not Ready / Unscheduled Patient arrived earlier than promised time. Acknowledge the wait, expedite order in queue, give a realistic updated time, and invite patient to sit comfortably.

Module 5 • 20 Minutes Target

Workflow Capacity Calculations & Operational Case Lab

Throughput rate, queue delay estimations, wait time formulas, and staffing efficiency metrics.

1. Prescription Throughput Rate (\(TR\)) Formula

Throughput rate measures the total number of prescriptions processed through a station or whole pharmacy per hour:

\(TR \text{ (Rx/hr)} = \frac{\text{Total Prescriptions Completed}}{\text{Total Hours Worked}}\)
Sample Problem: A filling technician fills 180 prescriptions during a 6-hour shift. Calculate the technician's hourly throughput rate ($TR$).
\(TR = \frac{180\text{ Rx}}{6\text{ hours}} = 30\text{ Rx/hour}\)

2. Queue Wait Time Estimation Formula

Predicting estimated patient wait time based on queue length and processing capacity prevents customer dissatisfaction:

\(\text{Estimated Wait Time (min)} = \frac{\text{Prescriptions in Backlog Queue}}{\text{Team Filling Rate (Rx/min)}}\)
Sample Problem: There are 15 prescriptions waiting in the filling queue. The pharmacy filling station processes 0.5 prescriptions per minute ($30\text{ Rx/hr}$). What estimated wait time should be quoted to a waiting patient?
\(\text{Wait Time} = \frac{15\text{ Rx}}{0.5\text{ Rx/min}} = 30\text{ minutes}\)
