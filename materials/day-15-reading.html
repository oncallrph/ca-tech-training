Module 1 • 20 Minutes Target

Medication Purchasing & Ordering Workflows

Primary wholesalers, Prime Vendor Agreements, automated ordering systems, and controlled drug ordering.

1. Procurement Channels in Pharmacy

Pharmacies source pharmaceuticals through three primary channels depending on cost structures, urgency, and manufacturer contracts:

Primary Wholesaler
Full-line distributors (e.g., AmerisourceBergen, Cardinal Health, McKesson). Provide next-day delivery for 90%+ of pharmacy stock under a Prime Vendor Agreement (PVA).
Direct Manufacturer
Purchasing directly from drug makers. Used primarily for specialized biologics, high-cost specialty drugs, or during major wholesaler stock shortages.
Secondary Wholesaler
Used to source hard-to-find medications or back-ordered items not currently stocked by the primary wholesaler.

2. Schedule II Controlled Substance Procurement

Ordering Schedule II (C-II) controlled substances requires strict federal tracking under DEA oversight:

DEA Form 222 (Paper) Traditional paper ordering form. Must be signed by the DEA registrant or individual holding Power of Attorney (POA). Copy 1 & 2 sent to supplier; pharmacy retains Copy 3. (Note: Single-sheet form standard adopted in 2019/2021).
CSOS (Electronic) Controlled Substance Ordering System. Digital equivalent utilizing PKI digital certificates. Allows ordering of C-II through C-V without paper forms, higher line-item limits, and faster delivery.

3. Receiving & Invoice Verification Steps

Upon receiving stock shipments, technicians must execute a strict 4-step intake protocol:

  1. Box Count & Physical Inspection: Verify tote/box counts match the courier manifest and inspect containers for damage or broken seals.
  2. Invoice Verification: Cross-check delivered drug name, NDC, strength, quantity, and lot number against the wholesale invoice packing slip.
  3. Refrigerated & Cold-Chain Check: Immediately unpack cold-chain items ($2^\circ\text{C}$ to $8^\circ\text{C}$ / $36^\circ\text{F}$ to $46^\circ\text{F}$) and verify temperature indicators before general stock.
  4. Stock Entry & Sign-Off: Update electronic perpetual inventory systems and file signed invoices (C-II invoices must be kept SEPARATELY from non-controlled invoices for 2 years in CA).

Module 2 • 25 Minutes Target

PAR Levels, Reorder Point (ROP) Math & Stock Rotation

Periodic vs. continuous systems, safety stock calculations, and FEFO/FIFO stock management rules.

1. PAR Levels & Minimum / Maximum Systems

Pharmacies maintain preset inventory thresholds to prevent stockouts while preventing over-investment of cash in excess inventory:

Core Inventory Terms:
  • PAR Level (Periodic Automatic Replacement): The target baseline quantity of a medication that should be on hand at any given time.
  • Minimum Level (Reorder Point): The lowest threshold quantity stock can reach before an automated reorder is triggered.
  • Maximum Level: The ceiling limit of stock to prevent overstocking and shelf clutter.
  • Safety Stock (\(SS\)): Buffer inventory held to protect against unexpected demand spikes or vendor delivery delays.

2. Reorder Point (ROP) Formula & Calculation

The Reorder Point (\(ROP\)) represents the stock level that triggers a purchase order. It accounts for daily usage rate, wholesaler lead time, and safety stock buffer:

\(ROP = (d \times L) + SS\)
\(d\) = Average Daily Usage (units/day)
\(L\) = Lead Time (days to arrive)
\(SS\) = Safety Stock (buffer units)
Sample Math Walkthrough:

A pharmacy dispenses an average of $20$ bottles of Atorvastatin $20\text{ mg}$ daily ($d = 20$). The wholesaler takes $2$ days to deliver ($L = 2$). The pharmacy maintains a safety stock buffer of $10$ bottles ($SS = 10$).

\(ROP = (20 \times 2) + 10 = 40 + 10 = 50\text{ bottles}\)

Conclusion: When stock drops to 50 bottles, an order MUST be placed!

3. Stock Placement & FEFO Rule

Proper shelf management prevents medication expiration losses. Pharmacies strictly enforce the FEFO (First Expired, First Out) protocol:

FEFO (First Expired, First Out)

Newly received shipments are placed BEHIND existing stock on the shelf. The bottles expiring earliest are pulled forward to be dispensed first.

FIFO vs. FEFO Distinctions

While FIFO (First In, First Out) uses arrival date, FEFO specifically prioritizes expiration date—critical when newer shipments arrive with shorter expiration dates than existing stock!

Module 3 • 20 Minutes Target

Controlled Substance Perpetual Inventory & Auditing

Schedule II perpetual logs, mandatory DEA biennial counts, discrepancy tracking, and DEA Form 106 theft reporting.

1. Schedule II Perpetual Inventory Maintenance

Federal and California regulations require continuous, real-time tracking of all Schedule II (C-II) controlled substances in a Perpetual Inventory Log:

1. Exact Count Requirement
C-II medications require an exact physical count for all inventory reconciliation, perpetual log balances, and biennial counts.
2. C-III through C-V Rule
Estimated counts are allowed for C-III to C-V containers holding under 1,000 units. Containers holding over 1,000 units require an exact count if opened.
Perpetual Log Formula:
\(\text{Ending Balance} = \text{Beginning Balance} + \text{Received Stock} - \text{Dispensed Quantity}\)

2. DEA Biennial Physical Inventory Mandate

Every licensed pharmacy must conduct a physical inventory of all controlled substances at least every two years (biennially):

Key DEA Biennial Inventory Rules:
  • Timing: Must be taken either at opening of business (AOB) or close of business (COB) on the inventory date.
  • Record Retention: Inventory records must be kept at the licensed location for at least 2 years (California law requires C-II records stored separately).
  • Newly Scheduled Drugs: When a non-controlled drug is reclassified as a controlled substance, a physical inventory of that drug must be conducted on the effective date of scheduling.

3. Theft & Significant Loss Reporting (DEA Form 106)

When a theft or significant loss of controlled substances occurs, specific reporting timelines must be met under federal and state law:

DEA Form 106 Protocol

The pharmacy must notify the DEA Field Division in writing within 1 business day of discovery of theft or significant loss. DEA Form 106 must be completed and submitted detailing the lost drugs, NDCs, and circumstances.

Module 4 • 20 Minutes Target

Drug Recalls, Expiration Management & Reverse Distributors

FDA recall classification levels, short-dated pull schedules, reverse distributors, and DEA Form 41 destruction rules.

1. FDA Drug Recall Classifications

When a drug product is defective or poses a health risk, the FDA issues or oversees a drug recall classified into three severity tiers:

Recall Class Health Hazard Severity Example Real-World Scenario
Class I Recall High probability of serious adverse health consequences or death. Label mix-up involving potent concentrated heart medication or bacterial contamination in sterile IV fluids.
Class II Recall May cause temporary or medically reversible adverse health consequences. Sub-potent oral tablet dosage or minor impurity within allowable safety margins.
Class III Recall Unlikely to cause adverse health consequences. Packaging typo, container color variation, or minor labeling formatting error.

2. Expiration Date Interpretation & Pull Schedules

Medication expiration dates represent the last date the manufacturer guarantees full potency and safety:

Month/Year Rule

If an expiration date lists only month and year (e.g., 08/2026), the medication is valid through the LAST DAY of that month (August 31, 2026).

Short-Dated Pull Schedule

Pharmacies routinely pull medications expiring within 3 to 6 months from active inventory and move them to a designated "Short-Date Quarantine" shelf for return or disposal.

3. Disposal via Reverse Distributors (DEA Form 41)

Pharmacies cannot simply throw away or flush expired medications. They utilize specialized DEA-registered entities:

Reverse Distributor Process:
  • Controlled Substance Destruction: Controlled drugs are transferred to a reverse distributor, who processes DEA Form 41 (Registrant Record of Controlled Substances Destroyed).
  • C-II Transfer: Transferring Schedule II drugs to a reverse distributor requires issuing a DEA Form 222 from the reverse distributor to the pharmacy.

Module 5 • 20 Minutes Target

Inventory Performance Metrics & Financial Math Lab

Inventory Turnover Ratio (ITR), Days Inventory Outstanding (DIO), shrinkage, and gross margin calculations.

1. Inventory Turnover Ratio (ITR)

The Inventory Turnover Ratio (\(ITR\)) measures how many times a pharmacy sells and replaces its stock over a given period (usually 1 year). Higher turnover indicates efficient stock management without tied-up capital:

\(ITR = \frac{\text{Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)}}{\text{Average Inventory Value}}\)
Sample Problem: A retail pharmacy has annual Cost of Goods Sold (\(COGS\)) of $\$2,400,000$ and maintains an Average Inventory Value of $\$200,000$.
\(ITR = \frac{\$2,400,000}{\$200,000} = 12\text{ turns per year}\)

2. Days Inventory Outstanding (DIO)

Days Inventory Outstanding (\(DIO\)) measures the average number of days it takes to turn inventory into sales:

\(DIO = \frac{365}{ITR}\)
Using the previous ITR of 12 turns/year:
\(DIO = \frac{365}{12} \approx 30.4\text{ days}\)

Interpretation: On average, stock sits on the shelf for approximately 30 days before being sold.

3. Inventory Shrinkage Calculations

Shrinkage represents lost inventory value due to theft, damage, expiration, or administrative errors:

Shrinkage Formula:
\(\text{Shrinkage} = \text{Book Inventory Value} - \text{Physical Inventory Value}\)
