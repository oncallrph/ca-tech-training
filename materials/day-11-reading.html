Module 1 of 6 • Estimated Reading Time: 20 Minutes Packaging Materials

Packaging Materials, Container Types & USP <659> Standards

Pharmaceutical packaging is defined as the container, closure, and exterior carton that holds, protects, and delivers a drug product. Primary packaging directly contacts the medication (e.g., amber vial, blister pack, glass ampule), while secondary packaging holds primary containers (e.g., outer cardboard box).

1.1 Primary Packaging Materials: Glass vs. Plastics

Glass Containers

USP Glass Types I, II & III

Glass is impermeable to moisture and gases, heat resistant, and highly chemical-resistant.

  • Type I (Borosilicate): Highly resistant; required for parenterals & IV solutions.
  • Type II (Treated Soda-Lime): Suitable for acidic/neutral aqueous parenterals.
  • Type III (Soda-Lime): Used for non-aqueous parenterals & dry powders.
Plastic Polymers

HDPE, PET & Polypropylene

Plastics are lightweight, shatterproof, and cost-effective, but may allow gas permeability or leaching.

  • HDPE: Standard for solid oral stock bottles and amber prescription vials.
  • PET / PETG: Clear plastic used for oral liquid bottles.
  • Polypropylene: Autoclavable plastic used for syringe bodies & closures.

1.2 USP <659> Container Definitions & Light Protection

United States Pharmacopeia (USP) General Chapter **<659>** defines the specific performance metrics required for pharmaceutical containers:

Well-Closed

Protects contents from extraneous solids and loss of drug under normal handling and storage conditions.

Tight Container

Protects contents from contamination by liquids, solids, or vapors, and prevents effervescence or deliquescence.

Hermetic Container

Impervious to air or any other gas under customary conditions. Standard for sterile injectables & ampules.

Light-Resistant

Amber glass/plastic that limits light transmission between 290nm and 450nm to less than 10%.

Module 2 of 6 • Estimated Reading Time: 25 Minutes Child-Resistant Packaging

Poison Prevention Packaging Act (PPPA) & Waiver Protocols

Enacted in 1970 and enforced by the **Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC)**, the **Poison Prevention Packaging Act (PPPA)** requires child-resistant (C-R) packaging for almost all oral prescription drugs and hazardous OTC products to protect children under 5 years of age from accidental ingestion.

2.1 C-R Testing Standards & Re-Use Restrictions

Performance Criteria:

To qualify as child-resistant, at least **80%** of tested children under 5 years old must be unable to open the container within 10 minutes, while at least **90%** of tested adults must easily open and reclose it.

Re-Use Rule:

Pharmacy technicians **MUST NOT reuse plastic prescription vials or C-R caps** when dispensing refills, because plastic stress leads to loss of safety integrity. Glass bottles may be reused IF a new plastic cap is provided.

2.2 Patient Blanket Waivers vs. Prescriber Waivers

Legal Waiver Mandates (Critical Exam Rule)
Patient Request (Blanket Waiver):

A patient may request a **blanket waiver** requesting non-child-resistant (easy-open) caps on ALL current and future prescriptions. Signed written documentation is strongly recommended for pharmacy risk records.

Prescriber Request (Single Rx Waiver):

A physician/prescriber can request non-C-R caps **ONLY on an individual prescription basis**. Prescribers **CANNOT** issue a blanket waiver for all of a patient's prescriptions or refills.

2.3 Statutory Exemptions from PPPA

Certain oral medications are legally **EXEMPT** from child-resistant packaging requirements because immediate access during an emergency is critical, or because dose packaging poses low risk:

  • Sublingual Nitroglycerin (SL NTG): Emergency angina treatment (must remain in original glass bottle).
  • Sublingual & Chewable Isosorbide Dinitrate: Doses of 10 mg or less.
  • Oral Contraceptives: Memory-pack dial dispensers.
  • Corticosteroid Dose Packs: E.g., Methylprednisolone (Medrol Dosepak), Prednisone dose packs.
  • Anhydrous Cholestyramine Powder & Potassium Supplements: Unit-dose packets & effervescent tablets (<50 mEq per dose).
  • Aerosol Inhalers: Metered Dose Inhalers (MDIs) for asthma emergency relief.

Module 3 of 6 • Estimated Reading Time: 20 Minutes Label Anatomy

Prescription Label Anatomy, California Board Rules & Auxiliary Labels

Prescription labels must clearly convey all vital patient directions. Under **California Business & Professions Code 4076**, California enforces patient-centered label standards to minimize medication errors.

3.1 California 10-Point Bold Prominence Rule (B&P Code 4076)

In California, the following **4 Critical Elements** must comprise at least **50% of the total label area**, printed in 10-point bold sans-serif font:

Critical Element 1
Patient Name
First & Last Name of Patient
Critical Element 2
Drug Name & Strength
Generic + Manufacturer or Brand Name
Critical Element 3
Directions for Use
Explicit Patient Instructions (Sig)
Critical Element 4
Condition / Purpose
If included on prescription order

3.2 Additional CA Mandated Elements & Physical Description

In addition to the 4 critical elements, California prescription labels must include:

  • Pharmacy Name, Address & Phone Number
  • Prescription (Rx) Number
  • Prescriber Name
  • Date of Dispensing / Filling
  • Quantity Dispensed
  • Expiration Date / Beyond-Use Date (BUD)
  • Physical Description of Drug: Color, shape, and imprint code (e.g., "Yellow, oval tablet stamped 'A 01'").
  • Number of Refills Remaining
  • Cautionary Auxiliary Warning Labels

3.3 Essential Auxiliary Warning Labels

Shake Well Before Use

Mandatory for all oral & topical suspensions to ensure uniform active drug distribution.

Refrigerate • Do Not Freeze

Required for reconstitutable antibiotics (e.g., Amoxicillin/Clavulanate, Augmentin), insulins, and biologicals.

Opioid / Controlled Risk

Required for Schedule II-V opioids: "Caution: Federal law prohibits transfer to any person other than patient."

Module 4 of 6 • Estimated Reading Time: 20 Minutes Tamper-Evident & Repackaging

Tamper-Evident Packaging & Unit-Dose Repackaging Rules

In 1982, following the tragic Tylenol cyanide tampering incidents in Chicago, Congress passed the **Federal Anti-Tampering Act**. This act mandates tamper-evident packaging for consumer OTC drug products and cosmetic items.

4.1 Tamper-Evident Technologies

A tamper-evident package is equipped with one or more indicators or barriers to entry which, if breached or missing, provide visible evidence to consumers that tampering has occurred:

Induction Seals

Foil seals heat-sealed across container mouth beneath screw cap.

Breakable Ring Caps

Plastic/metal cap ring that breaks away upon initial unscrewing.

Blister Packaging

Medication sealed between thermoformed plastic bubble & foil backing.

Shrink Bands

Cellophane bands heat-shrunk around outer cap-to-bottle junction.

4.2 Unit-Dose Repackaging & Expiration BUD Standards

Hospital and institutional pharmacies frequently repackage oral solid medications from commercial bulk bottles into single unit-dose blister cards (Bingo cards) or foil packets.

USP Unit-Dose Beyond-Use Date (BUD) Calculation Rule

Under USP packaging standards, the maximum Beyond-Use Date (BUD) assigned to repackaged solid oral dosage forms is:

BUD = 1 Year OR 25% of the remaining manufacturer expiration date on the bulk bottle (WHICHEVER IS SHORTER, not to exceed 1 year).
