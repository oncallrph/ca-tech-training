Module 1 • 20 Minutes Target

OTC Regulation & Behind-the-Counter (BTC) Sales

FDA OTC Drug Monographs, Drug Facts Label standards, and Combat Methamphetamine Epidemic Act (CMEA) enforcement.

1. Regulatory Framework for Over-the-Counter Drugs

The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) regulates OTC drugs to ensure they are safe and effective for self-administration without a prescription. OTC drugs are approved via two primary pathways: the OTC Drug Monograph Process (a "recipe book" of pre-approved ingredients, doses, and labeling) or a New Drug Application (NDA).

2. The Standardized "Drug Facts" Label

Federal law mandates a uniform Drug Facts Label on all non-prescription medications to ensure patient safety and readability:

Drug Facts (Standard Format)
Active Ingredient(s): Purpose & amount per dosage unit.
Uses: Indications treated or symptoms managed.
Warnings: When not to use, drug interactions, side effects.
Directions: Specific dosage, frequency, and duration by age/weight.
Other Information: Storage guidelines, sodium/calcium content.
Inactive Ingredients: Preservatives, colors, binders, flavorings.

3. Behind-the-Counter (BTC) Drugs & CMEA Regulations

While the U.S. federal framework primarily recognizes prescription and non-prescription classes, a third functional category called Behind-the-Counter (BTC) exists for non-prescription medications subject to sales restrictions. The primary example is regulated under the Combat Methamphetamine Epidemic Act of 2005 (CMEA) for precursor chemicals: Pseudoephedrine (PSE), Ephedrine, and Phenylpropanolamine (PPA).

CMEA Regulatory Provision Legal Requirement & Threshold Limit Technician Operational Duty
Daily Retail Sales Limit $3.6\text{ g}$ base chemical per purchaser per day Enforce strict maximum daily quantity per customer.
30-Day Retail Limit $9.0\text{ g}$ base chemical per 30-day period Verify electronic logbook for 30-day cumulative limit.
30-Day Mail-Order Limit $7.5\text{ g}$ base chemical per 30-day period Apply strict mail-order shipping quantity caps.
Storage & Access Behind counter or in locked cabinet Keep products inaccessible to direct customer access.
Logbook & Purchaser ID Government photo ID + signature + log entry Check valid photo ID, record name/address/date/time; retain log for 2 years.

Module 2 • 25 Minutes Target

Major OTC Medication Categories & Active Ingredients

Therapeutic breakdown of self-care agents across major body systems.

1. Analgesics & Antipyretics (Pain & Fever)

Analgesics are among the most frequently purchased OTC drugs. Technicians must understand the key distinctions between non-NSAID analgesics and NSAIDs:

Active Ingredient Brand Example(s) Drug Class & Mechanism Key Safety Note / Limit
Acetaminophen (APAP) Tylenol Non-NSAID Analgesic / Antipyretic; central COX inhibition. Max $4000\text{ mg/day}$ ($3000\text{ mg}$ suggested); Risk of severe liver damage (hepatotoxicity).
Ibuprofen Advil, Motrin NSAID; non-selective COX-1 & COX-2 inhibitor. Take with food; risk of GI upset/ulcers & renal impairment. Max OTC $1200\text{ mg/day}$.
Naproxen Sodium Aleve Long-acting NSAID (dosed q8-12h). GI mucosal risk; avoid mixing with other NSAIDs.
Aspirin (Acetylsalicylic Acid) Bayer, Ecotrin Irreversible COX inhibitor; antiplatelet effect. Black Box: NEVER use in children $<19$ years with viral infection (Reye's Syndrome risk).

2. Gastrointestinal Agents

Antacids Calcium carbonate (Tums), Magnesium hydroxide (Milk of Magnesia). Neutralizes stomach acid immediately. Short duration.
H2 Receptor Blockers Famotidine (Pepcid AC). Reduces acid production. Onset 30–45 mins; lasts 10–12 hours.
Proton Pump Inhibitors Omeprazole (Prilosec OTC), Esomeprazole (Nexium 24HR). Blocks acid pumps; taken daily for 14 days for frequent heartburn.

3. Allergy, Cold & Respiratory Products

Antihistamines (1st vs 2nd Gen)
  • 1st Generation (Sedating): Diphenhydramine (Benadryl), Chlorpheniramine. Causes significant drowsiness, anticholinergic side effects.
  • 2nd Generation (Non-sedating): Loratadine (Claritin), Cetirizine (Zyrtec), Fexofenadine (Allegra). Preferred for daytime allergic rhinitis.
Decongestants, Antitussives & Expectorants
  • Decongestants: Phenylephrine (PE) OTC, Pseudoephedrine (Sudafed) BTC. Vasoconstrictors—causes elevated blood pressure!
  • Antitussives (Cough Suppressant): Dextromethorphan (DM).
  • Expectorants (Mucus Thinner): Guaifenesin (Mucinex).

Module 3 • 20 Minutes Target

Technician Scope of Practice, SCHOLAR-MAC & Referral Triage

Boundaries of technician communication, structured data gathering, and identifying critical referral red flags.

1. Technician Scope of Practice vs. Pharmacist Clinical Consultation

Under California state law and pharmacy regulations, pharmacy technicians are legally prohibited from providing clinical advice, recommending OTC drugs, or evaluating symptoms. However, technicians play a vital operational role by gathering intake information and directing the patient to the pharmacist.

Permitted Technician Duties
  • Guide patients to the physical shelf location of an OTC item.
  • Collect demographic, allergy, and current medication data.
  • Read package directions or price aloud directly from the box.
  • Refer all product selection or clinical questions immediately to the pharmacist.
Prohibited Technician Actions
  • Recommending a specific brand or active ingredient for symptoms.
  • Assessing whether a product is safe for a patient's medical condition.
  • Advising a patient to change prescribed doses or take off-label doses.
  • Answering "Which product works best for my cough?" without pharmacist handoff.

2. The SCHOLAR-MAC Information Gathering Framework

Technicians use the standardized SCHOLAR-MAC mnemonic to systematically collect information for the pharmacist evaluation:

Letter Mnemonic Element Information to Gather from Patient
SSymptomsWhat are the main symptoms the patient is experiencing?
CCharacteristicsWhat do the symptoms feel like (e.g., sharp pain, dry cough, throbbing)?
HHistoryWhat has been done so far? Have they tried other OTC products?
OOnsetWhen did the symptoms start?
LLocationWhere is the symptom located on or in the body?
AAggravating FactorsWhat makes the symptoms worse?
RRelieving FactorsWhat makes the symptoms better?
MMedicationsWhat prescription, OTC, and herbal products are currently taken?
AAllergiesAre there any known drug, food, or environmental allergies?
CConditionsWhat underlying medical conditions exist (e.g., HTN, Diabetes, Asthma)?

3. Red Flag Triage Criteria (Immediate Pharmacist / ER Handoff)

High-Risk Symptoms Requiring Immediate Clinical Handoff:
  • Chest pain, shortness of breath, or difficulty breathing.
  • High fever in infants under 3 months of age ($> 100.4^\circ\text{F}$ or $38.0^\circ\text{C}$).
  • Blood in stool, vomit, or sputum.
  • Pregnant or breastfeeding patients seeking medication advice.
  • Severe, sudden-onset visual changes or severe head trauma.

Module 4 • 20 Minutes Target

OTC Safety Concerns, Overlapping Ingredients & High-Risk Groups

Preventing accidental overdose, ingredient duplication, and adverse reactions in vulnerable populations.

1. Duplicate Active Ingredients & Acetaminophen Toxicity

A major hazard in OTC self-care is accidental duplication of active ingredients. Many combination OTC products (e.g., NyQuil, DayQuil, Excedrin, Percocet Rx) contain Acetaminophen (APAP). When patients take multiple combination products simultaneously, total daily APAP intake can exceed safe thresholds.

Acetaminophen Daily Maximum Thresholds:
  • Maximum Absolute Federal FDA Limit: $4000\text{ mg/day}$ (from all combined Rx and OTC sources).
  • Recommended Manufacturer / Clinical Limit: $3000\text{ mg/day}$ for long-term use or in patients with alcohol use / mild liver dysfunction.
  • Toxic Consequence: Severe, irreversible hepatic necrosis / liver failure.

2. Vulnerable Populations & High-Risk Contraindications

Pediatric Warnings Aspirin Risk: Reye's Syndrome (fatal encephalopathy & liver damage). Always use weight-based dosing ($mg/kg$) rather than age-based estimates.
Geriatric Warnings Beers Criteria OTC List: First-generation antihistamines (Diphenhydramine). Causes severe anticholinergic effects, confusion, urinary retention, and fall risk.
Disease Contraindications Hypertension (HTN): Decongestants (Pseudoephedrine, Phenylephrine). Causes systemic vasoconstriction and dangerous blood pressure spikes.

Module 5 • 20 Minutes Target

Clinical Calculations & OTC Case Lab

Weight-based pediatric dose calculations, volume determinations, and CMEA purchase math.

1. Weight-Based Pediatric Dosage Formula

Pediatric OTC medications must be dosed according to patient weight in kilograms ($kg$), using the conversion factor $1\text{ kg} = 2.2\text{ lbs}$:

$$\text{Weight (kg)} = \frac{\text{Weight (lbs)}}{2.2}$$ $$\text{Dose (mg)} = \text{Weight (kg)} \times \text{Dose Rate (mg/kg)}$$ $$\text{Volume (mL)} = \frac{\text{Dose (mg)} \times \text{Container Volume (mL)}}{\text{Container Mass (mg)}}$$
Worked Sample Problem: A child weighs $33\text{ lbs}$. The recommended OTC Acetaminophen dose is $15\text{ mg/kg}$ per dose. Liquid Children's Tylenol concentration is $160\text{ mg} / 5\text{ mL}$. What volume in $\text{mL}$ should be administered per dose?
Step 1 (Convert Weight): $33\text{ lbs} \div 2.2 = 15\text{ kg}$
Step 2 (Calculate mg Dose): $15\text{ kg} \times 15\text{ mg/kg} = 225\text{ mg}$
Step 3 (Calculate mL Volume): $\frac{225\text{ mg} \times 5\text{ mL}}{160\text{ mg}} = \frac{1125}{160} = 7.03\text{ mL} \approx 7\text{ mL}$

2. CMEA Quantity Math Verification

Calculating equivalent base weights for pseudoephedrine sales compliance:

Sample CMEA Math: A customer wants to buy 2 boxes of Sudafed 24-Hour containing 10 tablets per box. Each tablet contains $240\text{ mg}$ of Pseudoephedrine HCl (equivalent to $196.6\text{ mg}$ base). Does this sale comply with the $3.6\text{ g/day}$ limit?
Total Tablets = $2 \times 10 = 20\text{ tablets}$
Total Base Mass = $20 \times 196.6\text{ mg} = 3932\text{ mg} = 3.932\text{ g}$ base chemical
Result: DENIED. $3.932\text{ g}$ exceeds the legal daily limit of $3.6\text{ g}$.
