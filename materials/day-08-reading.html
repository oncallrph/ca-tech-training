Module 1 of 6 • Estimated Reading Time: 20 Minutes Legislative & Regulatory Framework

USP Standards (<795>, <797>, <800>) & California Regulations

In pharmaceutical practice, **compounding** is legally distinguished from manufacturing. While manufacturing involves the large-scale production of drug products regulated directly by the FDA under Current Good Manufacturing Practices (cGMP), compounding is a patient-specific healthcare service regulated primarily by state boards of pharmacy (such as the **California State Board of Pharmacy**) alongside federal oversight under Section 503A of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act.

1.1 The United States Pharmacopeia (USP) Standards

The United States Pharmacopeia is an independent, non-profit organization that establishes legally enforceable standards for medication identity, strength, quality, and purity. Chapters numbered below <1000> are strictly enforceable by law and regulatory inspectors.

USP <795>

Non-Sterile Compounding

Applies to non-sterile formulations including oral liquids, ointments, creams, suppositories, capsules, and troches. Defines three complexity levels:

  • Simple: Following USP monograph or official recipe (e.g., Captopril oral solution).
  • Moderate: Requires special calculations or unit mixing (e.g., Morphine suppository).
  • Complex: Requires complex processes or modified-release (e.g., Extended-release transdermal gel).
USP <797>

Sterile Compounding

Governs preparations delivered into the bloodstream, eyes, or spinal fluid (IV bags, TPNs, eye drops, epidurals).

  • Category 1 CSPs: Prepared in a Segregated Compounding Area (SCA) with shorter BUDs.
  • Category 2 CSPs: Prepared in an ISO Class 7 cleanroom suite with longer BUDs.
  • Category 3 CSPs: High-risk preparations requiring additional stability, sterility, and pyrogen testing.
USP <800>

Hazardous Drug Handling

Protects personnel, patients, and the environment when receiving, storing, compounding, and administering Hazardous Drugs (HDs).

  • NIOSH List: Group 1 (Antineoplastics), Group 2 (Non-antineoplastics), Group 3 (Reproductive toxins).
  • Negative Pressure: C-PEC hoods kept in negative-pressure room (-0.01 to -0.03 inches w.g.).
  • CSTDs: Closed-system drug transfer devices mandatory for administration.

1.2 Quality Assurance, Documentation & Master Formulation Records

Every compounded medication must be traceable, reproducible, and verifiable. In California, every compounding pharmacy must maintain two critical documents for every batch:

Master Formulation Record (The Recipe)

The official, static document containing step-by-step instructions, ingredients, exact quantities, equipment needed, storage conditions, container types, and Beyond-Use Date rationale.

Compounding Record (The Log)

The dynamic, batch-specific log recording actual manufacturer lot numbers, expiration dates, measured weights, technician signature, pharmacist review, and actual yield.

Module 2 of 6 • Estimated Reading Time: 25 Minutes Laboratory Equipment & Compounding Techniques

Equipment Mechanics, Dosage Forms & Core Techniques

Precision in pharmaceutical compounding relies heavily on understanding the mechanical limitations of equipment, chemical compatibility of materials, and proper physical manipulation techniques.

2.1 Measurement Balances & Minimum Weighable Quantity (MWQ)

Compounding pharmacies utilize two primary types of balances:

  • Class III (Class A) Torsion Balance: Uses a mechanical beam and internal spring. Has a Sensitivity Requirement (SR) of $6\,\text{mg}$. This means adding $6\,\text{mg}$ causes the indicator pointer to shift by 1 index division.
  • Electronic Digital Analytical Balance: Uses electromagnetic force restoration. Highly accurate up to $0.1\,\text{mg}$ or $1\,\text{mg}$.
Minimum Weighable Quantity (MWQ) Standard Formula

To ensure that measurement error never exceeds the USP standard maximum allowable error of $5\%$ ($0.05$), the Minimum Weighable Quantity ($MWQ$) on a mechanical Class III balance is calculated as:

$$MWQ = \frac{\text{Sensitivity Requirement (SR)}}{\text{Maximum Allowable Error \%}} = \frac{6\,\text{mg}}{0.05} = 120\,\text{mg}$$

Key Clinical Takeaway: Never weigh an amount smaller than $120\,\text{mg}$ directly on a Class III torsion balance with $SR = 6\,\text{mg}$ unless using an aliquot method.

2.2 Mortars, Pestles & Volumetric Glassware Selection

Glass Mortar & Pestle

Smooth, non-porous glass surface. Ideal for preparing liquids, suspensions, oily compounds, and materials that stain easily (e.g., Iodine, Methylene Blue). Does not triturate powders effectively because it lacks surface friction.

Wedgewood & Porcelain Mortar

Unglazed or rough ceramic interior. Provides high surface friction necessary for trituration (grinding coarse powders into fine particles). Porcelain is slightly smoother than Wedgewood and easier to sanitize.

Graduated Cylinders

Tall, uniform cylindrical shape providing high volumetric precision. Always read liquid volume at eye level at the bottom of the meniscus. Choose a cylinder where liquid volume fills more than $20\%$ of total capacity.

Conical Graduates

Wide mouth and tapering sides. Less precise than cylindrical graduates, but allows easy insertion of stirring rods for dissolving viscous liquids or thick suspensions.

2.3 Five Fundamental Non-Sterile Compounding Techniques

1. Geometric Dilution: A critical technique used when blending a small amount of potent active ingredient with a large amount of inactive diluent powder. Equal parts of diluent are added incrementally to the active powder and mixed thoroughly at each step to ensure uniform drug distribution throughout the mixture.
2. Trituration: The continuous rubbing or grinding of a solid substance in a mortar with a pestle to reduce particle size to a fine powder without chemical change.
3. Levigation: The process of reducing particle size of a solid powder by triturating it with a small amount of insoluble liquid vehicle (a levigating agent such as mineral oil for hydrophobic bases or glycerin for hydrophilic bases) to form a smooth paste before incorporation into an ointment base.
4. Spatulation: Blending powders or creams together on an ointment slab using a flexible spatula. No particle size reduction occurs during spatulation.
5. Pulverization by Intervention: Dissolving a gummy or hard crystalline solid (e.g., Camphor, Menthol) in a volatile solvent (such as Alcohol or Ether) and triturating until the solvent evaporates, leaving behind finely pulverized particles.

Module 3 of 6 • Estimated Reading Time: 20 Minutes Cleanroom Architecture & Garbing

Cleanroom Protocols, Air Quality & Garbing Sequence

Human operators are the primary source of particle shedding and microbial contamination in cleanroom environments. USP <797> establishes strict engineering controls and personal protective equipment (PPE) guidelines to prevent contamination during sterile compounding.

3.1 Cleanroom Architecture & ISO Air Classifications

Cleanroom environments are categorized by international standard ISO 14644-1 based on maximum allowable particulate concentration per cubic meter of air:

ISO Class Cleanroom Area Type Max Particles ($\ge 0.5\,\mu\text{m} / \text{m}^3$) Air Changes Per Hour (ACPH)
ISO Class 5 Primary Engineering Control (PEC Hood) 3,520 Continuous HEPA flow
ISO Class 7 Buffer Area (Cleanroom Suite) 352,000 ≥ 30 ACPH
ISO Class 8 Ante-Room (Gowning & Washing Area) 3,520,000 ≥ 20 ACPH
Positive Pressure Differential (Non-Hazardous): Air flows outward from the buffer area (ISO 7) into the ante-room (ISO 8) to prevent dirty room air from entering the sterile compounding space (≥ +0.02 inches water gauge).
Negative Pressure Differential (Hazardous USP <800>): Air flows inward into the hazardous compounding buffer room to contain hazardous vapors and powders (-0.01 to -0.03 inches water gauge).

3.2 Hand Hygiene & Aseptic Scrub Standard Operating Procedure

  1. Remove all outer garments, cosmetics, jewelry, rings, watches, and ear piercings.
  2. Nails must be short and clean; artificial nails or polish are strictly prohibited.
  3. Wet hands and forearms up to the elbows under warm water.
  4. Clean fingernails under running water using a disposable nail cleaner pick.
  5. Apply antiseptic soap (e.g., Chlorhexidine or Povidone-Iodine) and scrub hands and forearms vigorously for at least 30 seconds.
  6. Rinse thoroughly under running warm water from fingertips towards elbows.
  7. Dry hands completely using lint-free disposable towels or warm air dryer.

Aseptic Techniques, Laminar Airflow & BUD Matrix

A **Primary Engineering Control (PEC)** is a device or room that provides an ISO Class 5 environment for compounding. These include Laminar Airflow Workbenches (LAFW), Class II Biological Safety Cabinets (BSC), and Compounding Aseptic Isolators (CAI).

4.1 Laminar Airflow Mechanics & First Air Principles

Air in a laminar flow workbench passes through a pre-filter to remove large dust particles, then is forced through a **HEPA (High-Efficiency Particulate Air) Filter** at a constant velocity of $90\,\text{feet per minute} \pm 20\%$. HEPA filters remove $99.97\%$ of airborne particles $0.3\,\mu\text{m}$ or larger.

Horizontal Laminar Flow Workbench (LAFW):

Air flows from the back filter horizontally forward towards the operator. Used only for non-hazardous sterile preparations. Never place hands between the HEPA filter (back) and the critical site.

Vertical Biological Safety Cabinet (BSC):

Air flows from the top HEPA filter downward toward the work surface and is pulled into front air grilles. Mandatory for hazardous drugs (USP <800>) to protect the operator.

Crucial Hood Manipulation Operating Rules
  • The 6-Inch Rule: All compounding manipulations must take place at least 6 inches inside the outer front edge of the hood.
  • First Air Integrity: Never block the uninterrupted HEPA-filtered air stream ("First Air") reaching critical sites (needle hubs, ampule necks, vial stoppers).
  • Sanitization Protocol: Disinfect the hood surface with 70% Sterile Isopropyl Alcohol (IPA) top-to-bottom, back-to-front in overlapping parallel strokes. Allow alcohol to air dry completely.
  • Blower Startup: If turned off, the hood must operate continuously for at least 30 minutes before compounding begins.

4.2 Beyond-Use Date (BUD) Determination Standards

A Beyond-Use Date (BUD) is the date or time after which a compounded preparation cannot be used, administered, or stored. It is calculated from the date/time of compounding.

USP <795> Non-Sterile BUD Guidelines (Absence of Specific Stability Data)

Formulation Type Maximum Beyond-Use Date (BUD) Storage Condition Requirements
Nonaqueous Formulations 6 Months (180 Days) Controlled Room Temperature or as stated in API expiration.
Water-Containing Oral Liquids 14 Days Must be stored under refrigeration ($2^\circ\text{C}$ to $8^\circ\text{C}$).
Water-Containing Topical Formulations 30 Days Controlled Room Temperature ($20^\circ\text{C}$ to $25^\circ\text{C}$).

Compounding Mathematics & Formula Calculations

Calculation errors in pharmacy compounding can lead to severe adverse drug events. Technicians must master concentration conversions, dilution principles ($C_1 V_1 = C_2 V_2$), and the Alligation Alternate method.

The Alligation Alternate Method Step-by-Step

The Alligation Alternate method is used when mixing two different strengths of stock solutions or ointments (a Higher % and a Lower %) to achieve a desired intermediate concentration (% Target).

[Higher %]              [Target % - Lower %] = Parts Higher
           [Target %]
[Lower %]               [Higher % - Target %] = Parts Lower
Sum of Parts = Total Parts
$$\text{Volume of Stock needed} = \left(\frac{\text{Parts of Stock}}{\text{Total Parts}}\right) \times \text{Total Target Volume}$$
