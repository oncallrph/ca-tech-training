Day 16: Medication Information & Drug Classifications

Welcome to Day 16 of your pharmacy technician training program. Mastery of drug classifications, reference literature, generic-brand stems, and drug interaction awareness is essential for preventing medication errors and assisting pharmacists safely. Today's curriculum covers drug reference hierarchies (FDA books), major organ system drug classes, generic drug naming conventions (USAN stems), Tall Man lettering, boxed warnings, narrow therapeutic index (NTI) drugs, and clinical calculations.

Module 1 • 20 Minutes Target

Drug Information Resources & Literature Hierarchy

Primary, secondary, and tertiary literature, official FDA reference books, and digital clinical decision tools.

1. Hierarchy of Information Sources

Drug information is categorized into three levels based on novelty, detail, and level of peer review:

Primary Literature
Original research studies, clinical trials, and journal articles (e.g., NEJM, JAMA). Most current, but requires critical appraisal.
Secondary Literature
Indexing and abstracting services that guide users to primary literature (e.g., PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane Library).
Tertiary Literature
Summarized, consolidated information from primary sources (e.g., textbooks, drug compendia, Facts & Comparisons, Micromedex). First-line resource for daily practice.

2. FDA & Standard Reference Books (Color Books)

Standard pharmacy reference books provide specialized drug data mandated by law and clinical practice guidelines:

Reference Guide Primary Focus & Contents Common Clinical Use Case
FDA Orange Book Approved Drug Products with Therapeutic Equivalence Evaluations. Verifying if generic drug A is AB-rated to substitute for brand drug B.
FDA Purple Book Lists licensed Biological Products and biosimilar/interchangeable evaluations. Determining if a biosimilar insulin or mAb can be interchanged.
Red Book Drug pricing information, Average Wholesale Price (AWP), NDCs, and packaging. Financial reimbursement and drug cost comparisons.
CDC Yellow Book Health Information for International Travel (vaccines, prophylaxis). Checking malaria prophylaxis rules for travel to endemic countries.
CDC Pink Book Epidemiology and Prevention of Vaccine-Preventable Diseases. Reviewing childhood and adult immunization schedules.
FDA Green Book Approved Animal Drug Products (veterinary pharmaceuticals). Checking animal dosage forms and safety regulations.

3. Clinical Compendia & Specialty References

Trissel's Handbook on Injectable Drugs The gold standard reference for IV drug compatibility, stability, physical dilution, and Y-site co-infusion data.
Briggs' Drugs in Pregnancy & Lactation Comprehensive reference for placental transfer, fetal risk factors, and breastmilk excretion data.
Goodman & Gilman's The Pharmacological Basis of Therapeutics—indepth clinical pharmacology and receptor mechanism text.
Sanford Guide Gold standard guidance for antimicrobial therapy, dosing, organism susceptibility, and antibiotic selection.

Module 2 • 25 Minutes Target

Major Therapeutic Drug Classifications

Organ-system classification, mechanisms of action, and high-frequency top-200 medications.

1. Cardiovascular System Agents

Cardiovascular drugs manage hypertension, heart failure, dyslipidemia, and thromboembolic disorders:

Sub-Class Mechanism of Action Generic (Brand) Examples
ACE Inhibitors Blocks conversion of Angiotensin I to II; reduces vasoconstriction. Lisinopril (Zestril), Enalapril (Vasotec)
ARBs Blocks Angiotensin II Type-1 receptors directly. Losartan (Cozaar), Valsartan (Diovan)
Beta Blockers Blocks $\beta_1$ adrenergic cardiac receptors; reduces heart rate & BP. Metoprolol (Lopressor/Toprol-XL), Atenolol (Tenormin)
Statins (HMG-CoA Reductase Inhibitors) Inhibits rate-limiting hepatic cholesterol synthesis enzyme. Atorvastatin (Lipitor), Simvastatin (Zocor), Rosuvastatin (Crestor)
Anticoagulants Inhibits clotting cascade factors (Xa or IIa) to prevent thrombi. Warfarin (Coumadin), Apixaban (Eliquis), Rivaroxaban (Xarelto)

2. Central Nervous System (CNS) & Metabolic Agents

CNS & Psychotropic Agents
  • SSRIs: Sertraline (Zoloft), Fluoxetine (Prozac), Escitalopram (Lexapro) – increases synaptic serotonin.
  • Benzodiazepines (C-IV): Alprazolam (Xanax), Lorazepam (Ativan) – enhances GABA activity.
  • Anticonvulsants: Gabapentin (Neurontin), Levetiracetam (Keppra).
Endocrine & Diabetes Agents
  • Biguanides: Metformin (Glucophage) – decreases hepatic glucose production.
  • Insulins: Rapid-acting (Lispro/Humalog), Long-acting (Glargine/Lantus).
  • Thyroid Hormone: Levothyroxine (Synthroid) – synthetic T4 replacement.

3. Anti-Infective & Gastrointestinal Agents

Key Anti-Infective Classes:
  • Penicillins & Cephalosporins: Amoxicillin (Amoxil), Cephalexin (Keflex) – inhibits bacterial cell wall synthesis.
  • Fluoroquinolones: Ciprofloxacin (Cipro), Levofloxacin (Levaquin) – inhibits bacterial DNA gyrase.
  • Macrolides: Azithromycin (Zithromax) – inhibits 50S ribosomal protein synthesis.
  • Proton Pump Inhibitors (PPIs): Omeprazole (Prilosec), Pantoprazole (Protonix) – suppresses gastric $H^+/K^+$ ATPase pump.

Module 3 • 20 Minutes Target

Generic USAN Stems, LASA Drugs & Tall Man Lettering

United States Adopted Names (USAN) stem rules, ISMP Look-Alike Sound-Alike error prevention, and Tall Man lettering standards.

1. Common USAN Generic Drug Suffixes (Stems)

Generic drug names contain official stems (prefixes or suffixes) designated by the USAN Council that identify the drug's therapeutic class and mechanism:

-pril (ACE Inhibitors) Lisinopril, Enalapril, Ramipril (Indication: Hypertension, Heart Failure).
-sartan (ARBs) Losartan, Valsartan, Olmesartan (Indication: Hypertension).
-olol (Beta Blockers) Atenolol, Metoprolol, Propranolol (Indication: Angina, Hypertension).
-statin (HMG-CoA Reductase Inhibitors) Atorvastatin, Simvastatin, Pravastatin (Indication: High Cholesterol).
-prazole (Proton Pump Inhibitors) Omeprazole, Pantoprazole, Esomeprazole (Indication: GERD, Ulcers).
-cillin (Penicillin Antibiotics) Amoxicillin, Ampicillin, Penicillin VK (Indication: Bacterial Infections).
-mab (Monoclonal Antibodies) Adalimumab, Infliximab, Rituximab (Indication: Autoimmune/Oncology).
-glinide / -gliptin (Antidiabetics) Repaglinide, Sitagliptin, Linagliptin (Indication: Type 2 Diabetes).

2. ISMP Look-Alike Sound-Alike (LASA) & Tall Man Lettering

To prevent dangerous mix-ups between look-alike drug names, the Institute for Safe Medication Practices (ISMP) and FDA mandate Tall Man Lettering (selective capitalization):

Tall Man Lettering Examples
hydrOXYzine (Antihistamine) vs. hydrALAZine (Antihypertensive)
vinBLAStine (Oncology) vs. vinCRIStine (Oncology)
BUSpirone (Anxiolytic) vs. buPROPrion (Antidepressant)
predniSONE (Steroid) vs. predniSOLONE (Steroid)

Module 4 • 20 Minutes Target

Drug Interactions, Warnings & Special Safety Concerns

Drug-drug, drug-food, and drug-disease interactions, FDA Boxed Warnings, and Narrow Therapeutic Index (NTI) drugs.

1. Major Drug-Drug & Drug-Food Interactions

Technicians must recognize high-risk drug interaction patterns during prescription entry to flag them for pharmacist review:

Interaction Pair / Agent Mechanism of Interaction Clinical Adverse Consequence
Warfarin + NSAIDs (Aspirin/Ibuprofen) Additive antiplatelet & mucosal erosion effect. Severe gastrointestinal hemorrhage / bleeding.
Grapefruit Juice + Statins (Atorvastatin/Simvastatin) Inhibits intestinal CYP3A4 enzyme breakdown. Increased serum statin levels, risking rhabdomyolysis.
ACE Inhibitor + Potassium Supplement ACE inhibitors reduce aldosterone, retaining $K^+$. Hyperkalemia leading to cardiac arrhythmias.
Tetracyclines/Fluoroquinolones + Dairy/Antacids Chelation bonding with $Ca^{2+}$, $Mg^{2+}$, $Fe^{2+}$ cations. Inactivation of antibiotic absorption; treatment failure.

2. FDA Boxed Warnings (Black Box Warnings)

An FDA Boxed Warning is the FDA's strictest warning for prescription drugs, alerting prescribers to serious, life-threatening risks:

Notable Black Box Warnings:
  • Fluoroquinolones (e.g., Ciprofloxacin): Risk of tendonitis and tendon rupture (Achilles tendon).
  • Antidepressants (SSRIs): Increased risk of suicidal thoughts in children, adolescents, and young adults ($< 24$ years).
  • Opioids + Benzodiazepines: Concomitant use causes severe respiratory depression, coma, and death.
  • Metformin: Risk of fatal Lactic Acidosis in severe renal impairment.

3. Narrow Therapeutic Index (NTI) Medications

NTI drugs have a narrow margin between therapeutic effectiveness and lethal toxicity. Small changes in dose or concentration lead to failure or severe toxicity:

Warfarin (Coumadin) Monitored via INR (target typically 2.0 - 3.0).
Digoxin (Lanoxin) Cardiac glycoside; toxicity causes visual halos & arrhythmias.
Levothyroxine (Synthroid) Monitored via TSH levels; precise mcg dosing.
Lithium (Eskalith) Mood stabilizer; toxicity causes tremors and renal failure.
Theophylline Bronchodilator; narrow blood level window.
Phenytoin (Dilantin) Anticonvulsant; saturable nonlinear kinetics.

Module 5 • 20 Minutes Target

Clinical Calculations & Drug Information Case Lab

Renal clearance estimation (Cockcroft-Gault), Body Surface Area (BSA) dosing, and dose screening math.

1. Creatinine Clearance (\(CrCl\)) Estimation (Cockcroft-Gault Formula)

Renally eliminated drugs (e.g., Gabapentin, Levofloxacin, Vancomycin) require dose adjustment based on estimated kidney function (\(CrCl\)):

\(CrCl \text{ (mL/min)} = \frac{(140 - \text{Age}) \times \text{Weight (kg)}}{72 \times SCr \text{ (mg/dL)}} \quad [\times 0.85 \text{ if Female}]\)
Sample Problem: Calculate the estimated $CrCl$ for a 60-year-old male weighing $72\text{ kg}$ with a Serum Creatinine ($SCr$) of $1.0\text{ mg/dL}$.
\(CrCl = \frac{(140 - 60) \times 72}{72 \times 1.0} = \frac{80 \times 72}{72} = 80\text{ mL/min}\)

2. Body Surface Area (BSA) Dosing Formula

Oncology chemotherapy and specialized pediatric regimens are calculated using Body Surface Area ($BSA$ in $m^2$) via Mosteller's formula:

\(BSA \text{ (m}^2\text{)} = \sqrt{\frac{\text{Height (cm)} \times \text{Weight (kg)}}{3600}}\)
Sample Problem: A pediatric patient has a $BSA$ of $1.2\text{ m}^2$. The prescribed chemotherapy dose is $150\text{ mg/m}^2/\text{day}$. What total daily dose in mg should be dispensed?
\(\text{Total Daily Dose} = 1.2\text{ m}^2 \times 150\text{ mg/m}^2 = 180\text{ mg}\)
