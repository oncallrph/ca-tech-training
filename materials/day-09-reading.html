Module 1 of 6 • Estimated Reading Time: 20 Minutes Regulatory Framework

Non-Sterile Compounding Scope & USP <795> Standards

Non-sterile pharmaceutical compounding involves preparing customized drug formulations that are not required to be completely sterile, yet must strictly meet standards for strength, quality, purity, and stability under **USP <795>**. Non-sterile preparations include oral solutions, suspensions, creams, ointments, gels, pastes, suppositories, troches, and hand-filled capsules.

1.1 Compounding Complexity Levels Under USP <795>

USP <795> categorizes non-sterile compounding into three distinct levels of technical complexity:

Simple Level

Monograph or Standard Recipe

Reconstituting or combining commercial products or following a standard USP monograph with clear stability data.

  • Example: Captopril oral solution
  • Example: Mixing two commercial OTC creams
Moderate Level

Specialized Calculations or Units

Requires special calculations, mixing multiple active ingredients, or compounding formulations without established stability data.

  • Example: Morphine suppositories
  • Example: Topical cream with custom API ratio
Complex Level

Advanced Processing Requirements

Compounds requiring specialized training, complex techniques, specialized physical facilities, or modified-release mechanisms.

  • Example: Extended-release transdermal gels
  • Example: Complex troche/pellet formulations

1.2 Facility Standards & Documentation Records

Non-sterile compounding requires a clean, designated area away from heavy pharmacy traffic. It must feature potable running water, purified water for compounding, adequate lighting, and easy-to-clean smooth surfaces.

Master Formulation Record

The master recipe detailing ingredient names, physical descriptions, exact quantities, equipment needed, detailed compounding procedures, container specifications, and BUD rationale.

Compounding Record (Log Sheet)

The batch-specific execution record capturing component lot numbers, expiration dates, actual measured weights, calculated yield, technician signature, and pharmacist verification.

Module 2 of 6 • Estimated Reading Time: 25 Minutes Laboratory Equipment

Measuring Ingredients, Balances & Volumetric Equipment

Accurate measurement of solid powders and liquid vehicles is mandatory for dosage uniformities in non-sterile preparations. Selecting the right equipment depends on the physical state, quantity, and compatibility of ingredients.

2.1 Prescription Balances & The Minimum Weighable Quantity (MWQ)

The standard mechanical Class III (Class A) Torsion Balance has a Sensitivity Requirement (SR) of $6\,\text{mg}$. According to USP rules, the maximum allowable measurement error on a balance is $5\%$ ($0.05$).

Minimum Weighable Quantity (MWQ) Equation
$$MWQ = \frac{\text{Sensitivity Requirement (SR)}}{\text{Acceptable Error Ratio}} = \frac{6\,\text{mg}}{0.05} = 120\,\text{mg}$$

The Solid Aliquot Method: If a recipe calls for less than $120\,\text{mg}$ of active drug powder on a Class III balance, the technician must weigh a larger quantity of active drug ($\ge 120\,\text{mg}$), dilute it homogeneously with an inert powder (such as lactose), and then weigh an aliquot portion of the mixture.

2.2 Mortar and Pestle Selection Guide

Glass Mortar

Smooth and non-porous. Best for mixing liquid solutions, oily suspensions, and chemicals that stain (e.g., Iodine). Poor for trituration.

Wedgewood Mortar

Rough, unglazed surface. High friction. Ideal for grinding coarse powders and crystalline solids into microfine particles.

Porcelain Mortar

Slightly porous glazed ceramic. Excellent for general powder blending, soft solids, and emulsification.

2.3 Volumetric Measurement Equipment

  • Graduated Cylinders: Preferred for accurate liquid measurement. Choose the smallest cylinder size that will accommodate the total volume (volume measured must equal at least $20\%$ of total cylinder capacity). Read at the bottom of the meniscus at eye level.
  • Conical Graduates: Tapered sides allow easy insertion of stirring rods for thick liquids, suspensions, or dissolving solids, but offer lower volumetric precision.
  • Calibrated Syringes & Pipettes: Used for highly accurate delivery of viscous or small liquid volumes ($< 5\,\text{mL}$).

Module 3 of 6 • Estimated Reading Time: 20 Minutes Compounding Procedures

Mixing and Compounding Creams, Ointments & Oral Forms

Mixing non-sterile compounds requires precise physical manipulation to ensure drug uniformity, smooth texture, and correct physical state.

3.1 Core Physical Compounding Techniques

1. Trituration: Grinding dry powder particles in a mortar with a pestle to reduce particle size and increase surface area.
2. Levigation: Wetting a dry powder with a small quantity of an insoluble liquid vehicle (a levigating agent like Mineral Oil for oleaginous bases, or Glycerin for aqueous/water-washable bases) to form a paste before blending into an ointment base.
3. Geometric Dilution: Mixing equal portions of a potent active ingredient powder with equal amounts of diluent powder in incremental steps to achieve completely homogeneous distribution.
4. Spatulation: Blending creams, ointments, or powders together on an ointment slab using a flexible steel or rubber spatula.
5. Fusion Method: Melting ingredients with high melting points (e.g., waxes, paraffin, hard base fats) over low heat before incorporating heat-sensitive ingredients.

3.2 Dosage Form Preparation Guidelines

Creams & Ointments:

Triturate active powder fine $\rightarrow$ Levigate with appropriate vehicle $\rightarrow$ Incorporate into ointment base using geometric dilution on an ointment slab $\rightarrow$ Package in ointment jar or tube.

Hard Gelatin Capsules:

Calculate total powder blend weight per capsule $\rightarrow$ Mix powders using geometric dilution $\rightarrow$ Use 'punch method' to fill capsule bodies $\rightarrow$ Check capsule weight tolerance ($\pm 5\%$).

Oral Solutions & Suspensions:

Solutions require complete dissolution in solvent. Suspensions require triturating insoluble drug with a wetting agent and suspending vehicle (e.g., Ora-Plus) to delay settling.

Suppositories:

Melt base (e.g., Cocoa Butter, PEG) $\rightarrow$ Incorporate active drug $\rightarrow$ Pour into mold $\rightarrow$ Allow to cool and solidify $\rightarrow$ Calculate density factor displacement.

Quality Assurance Controls & USP Beyond-Use Date Assignment

Quality control (QC) testing is required for every compounded batch before release. Technicians perform physical inspection and checks to ensure accuracy, stability, and patient safety.

4.1 Quality Control Checks for Non-Sterile Dosage Forms

Dosage Form Quality Control Physical Check Acceptance Criteria / Signs of Instability
Creams & Ointments Smoothness, texture, separation, odor check No phase separation, grittiness, bleeding, or discoloration
Oral Liquids pH test, color clarity, ease of redispersibility Suspensions must redisperse easily upon shaking; no caking
Hard Capsules Individual weight variation test ($\pm 5\%$) Capsules must be clean, uncracked, and uniform in color and fill weight
Suppositories Weight, surface appearance, melting point range No pitting, air bubbles, cracks, or softening at room temp

4.2 USP <795> Beyond-Use Date (BUD) Rules

In the absence of explicit published stability studies, the following standard maximum Beyond-Use Dates apply to non-sterile compounded preparations:

Nonaqueous Formulations
6 Months (180 Days)

Or the earliest expiration date of any Active Pharmaceutical Ingredient (API), whichever is shorter. Stored at room temp.

Water-Containing Oral Liquids
14 Days

Must be stored under cold refrigeration ($2^\circ\text{C}$ to $8^\circ\text{C}$ / $36^\circ\text{F}$ to $46^\circ\text{F}$).

Water-Containing Topicals
30 Days

Applies to creams, gels, and topical lotions containing water. Stored at controlled room temperature.

Non-Sterile Compounding Mathematics & Aliquot Calculations

Compounding mathematics requires precise execution of weight conversions, percentage concentration formulas, and solid/liquid aliquot methods when measuring small active ingredient quantities.

The Solid Aliquot Calculation Principle

When a required drug weight is less than the Minimum Weighable Quantity ($MWQ = 120\,\text{mg}$):

  1. Weigh a valid drug quantity ($D \ge 120\,\text{mg}$).
  2. Choose a diluent quantity ($X$) to create a total blend ($T = D + X$).
  3. Calculate aliquot quantity ($A \ge 120\,\text{mg}$): $\frac{\text{Required Drug}}{D} = \frac{A}{T}$.
